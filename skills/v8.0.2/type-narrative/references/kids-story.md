# Animated kids' story

## Purpose

Tell a simple, warm story for young children (about 2–8) that they can follow, enjoy and want again; often with a gentle lesson. Success: a child can retell what happened, names the character, and the parent finds it safe and calm.

## Format

- Bedtime/short story: 2–5 min. Social clip: 30–90 s. Episode-like: 5–7 min (high continuity cost).
- 16:9 for YouTube/TV; 9:16 for short clips. If posted to YouTube, it must be marked as made for kids where applicable.

## Structure

- **Character + home → want or problem → three tries (rule of three) → solution through kindness, cleverness or help → happy return home → gentle moral.**
- Use repetition: a refrain, a repeated action, a repeated line. Children enjoy predicting it.
- One problem, one setting change at most for under 3 years; two or three settings for older children.

## Scenes and shots

- 3 min: 5–7 scenes, 20–30 shots.
- Shot lengths 4–6 s; slower than adult content. Hold on the character's face when emotions change.
- Each try is a scene with the same shot pattern (approach, attempt, result, reaction) so the pattern is learnable.

## Pacing

- Slow and calm. Research on preschoolers links very fast cutting and frenetic fantasy to reduced attention afterwards; avoid rapid cuts and constant motion.
- Leave beats of stillness after jokes and questions.

## Must-have shots

- Character introduction: full body, clear face, in their home.
- Clear emotion close-ups (happy, sad, surprised, proud) with readable expressions.
- The problem made visible and simple.
- Each try with its result.
- The resolution and a warm group/hug shot (wide).
- Closing goodnight or goodbye image.

## What matters most

- Character appeal and absolute consistency (children notice).
- Clarity: one thing happening per shot, centred, big shapes.
- Emotional safety: no scary imagery, peril resolved quickly, no mockery.

## Narration, text and music

- Narrator-led; warm, slow, about 2 words per second (110–130 wpm), simple vocabulary, repeated phrases.
- Character voices optional; avoid lip-sync dependence.
- Gentle music; sound effects for actions. Avoid loud stingers.
- Title text and the moral as finishing text; never rely on generated letters, numbers or alphabet teaching.

## Common mistakes

- Too many characters and words.
- Adult humour or irony.
- Fast cuts and noise to "keep attention".
- Scary villains or unresolved peril.
- Inconsistent character design.

## Generation caveats

- Character drift is the top failure. Create a character sheet (front, side, back, expressions) and condition every shot on it. Keep the cast to 1–3 characters with distinct silhouettes and colours.
- Animals and creatures can gain extra limbs or change species features; prefer simple rounded designs, and inspect each clip.
- Teaching content (letters, numbers, counting, colours): generated letters and counts are often wrong. Add letters and numerals in finishing; count objects in keyframes and inspect.
- Uncanny or distorted faces frighten children; reject any frame with warped features rather than proceeding.
- Hugs and holding hands between characters may fuse; stage contact wide and brief.
- Do not use existing copyrighted characters.

## Intake questions

1. What age is the audience, and is there a lesson? (Default: 3–6, gentle lesson about the brief's theme.)
2. Who is the main character? Any existing character designs?
3. Length and where it will be watched? (Default: 3 min 16:9 bedtime story.)
4. Narrated story, or characters speaking? Which language?
