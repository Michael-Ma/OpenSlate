# Trailer / teaser

## Purpose

Make people want something that is not yet available: a film, series, game, book, product launch, event. Success: the viewer understands the premise or promise, feels the tone, wants more, and remembers the title and date.

## Format

- Teaser: 15–60 s. Trailer: 90–150 s. TV/social spots: 15–30 s.
- 16:9 or 2.39:1 letterboxed; 9:16 cut for social, re-composed rather than cropped.

## Structure

- **Teaser**: mood and mystery. A few striking images, one idea, no plot. Title card and date.
- **Trailer (three-act)**: Act 1 world and protagonist (30–40 s) → the inciting change; Act 2 escalation, stakes, montage of conflict (40–60 s); Act 3 peak montage, then a pause, a final beat (the "button": a joke, a scare, a line), then title and date.
- **Launch tease**: problem or mystery → glimpses (silhouette, detail, light) → reveal withheld or partial → date.
- Never reveal the ending or the twist.

## Scenes and shots

- Trailers are montage; scenes are acts. 2 min trailer: 30–40 shots, most 3–4 s (the shortest a shot can be). Teaser 30 s: 6–9 shots.
- Mix: establishing world shots, character close-ups, action peaks, title cards (finishing).
- Shots are at least 3 s, so a trailer's fastest rhythm comes from 3 s cuts plus motion peaks inside clips (a hit, a turn, a light flash); short teasers (5–8 shots) with strong motion work best.

## Pacing

- Slow open, accelerating cuts, hard stop of silence before the final beat, then title.
- Use rhythm: cut to beats, hits on reveals.

## Must-have shots

- An arresting opening image.
- The protagonist (or the product's silhouette) introduced in a single strong shot.
- The inciting moment.
- Two or three escalation images of scale or danger.
- The button shot.
- Title card and date frame (space for finishing text).

## What matters most

- Tone, unmistakably set by the first shots.
- Withholding: suggest more than you show.
- Title and date memorability.

## Narration, text and music

- Often uses dialogue lines or a narrator for premise; intertitles ("This summer") as finishing text.
- Music drives structure: quiet build, rises, drops, a cold silence before the button.

## Common mistakes

- Telling the whole plot.
- Monotone pace with no escalation.
- Weak final beat, or title card missing.
- Generic epic imagery unrelated to the actual work.

## Generation caveats

- If the trailer advertises an existing work, the images must be faithful to it (characters, designs, locations). Use supplied stills and character references; do not invent scenes that misrepresent the work.
- Character consistency across many short shots: lock references; keep the cast small in the plan.
- Dialogue on-face is unreliable; use voiceover over reaction shots and backs.
- Title, credits and date: finishing text only.
- Action peaks (explosions, fights, chases): one simple action per shot, motion towards or away from camera; cut around contact.
- For product launches, partial reveals (silhouette, detail, light sweep) also reduce fidelity risk until the full product reference is available.

## Intake questions

1. What is being promoted, and what should viewers feel and remember? (Title and date.)
2. Teaser or full trailer, and how long? (Default: 45 s teaser.)
3. What may be shown and what must stay hidden?
4. Do you have stills, character designs, a logline, dialogue lines or a track?
